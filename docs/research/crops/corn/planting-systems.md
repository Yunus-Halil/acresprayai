## 1. Row spacing

**United States and Canada.** The only acreage distribution is still Pioneer's. Read in full this time:
the share of corn acres in 30 in rows rose "from 80% in 2007 to nearly 86% in 2015"; 36 and 38 in rows have "declined,
comprising 7% of acres currently"; rows under 30 in are "about 6%" (6.3% in 2015, 4.2% in 2010); of those, in 2012, 20 in
3.0%, 22 in 2.6%, 15 in 0.7%. Source: "Pioneer Brand Concentration Survey", 2015, no sample size or method given
([Pioneer, Row Width in Corn Grain Production](https://www.pioneer.com/us/agronomy/row-width-grain-corn.html), vendor
survey). The same page maps "USDA-NASS farmer-reported row widths, 2013-2017" for 15, 20 and 22 in rows: Minnesota has
the highest narrow-row frequency, then South Dakota, and "narrow row implementation remains less than 5% in most of the
central Corn Belt states"; the map carries no numbers. Searches for a 2020 to 2023 survey found nothing newer. Nebraska's
2011 USDA survey (mean 31.3 in, 1.7% at 20.5 in or narrower) is in corn.md. The one later, self-selected sample is the
2020 Strip-Till Farmer benchmark: 84% of responding strip-tillers plant 30 in rows
([Strip-Till Farmer, 2020 study](https://www.striptillfarmer.com/articles/2050-strip-tilled-corn-acres-soybean-yields-increase), snippet).

**Twin rows.** "Less than 1 percent of all corn plantings" ([Pioneer, Twin-Row Corn Yielded Mixed Results](https://www.pioneer.com/us/agronomy/twin-row-corn.html),
vendor); a USDA-based figure of under 0.2% of the 2009 crop appeared only in a search summary (snippet). Geometry: Great
Plains describes twin row as two rows "about 7 to 8 inches apart on 30-inch centers" so a 30 in corn head takes both
([Farm Progress 2012, Case IH to sell twin-row planters from Great Plains](https://www.farmprogress.com/farming-equipment/case-ih-to-sell-twin-row-planters-from-great-plains), vendor via press);
UNL says "7 inches apart" ([UNL G2216](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g2216/2013/html/view)); a Great Plains
no-till trial used 7.5 in ([No-Till Farmer](https://www.no-tillfarmer.com/articles/3237-no-till-twin-rows-are-working), snippet).
In the furrow-irrigated South, LSU describes twin rows 9.5 in apart centred on 40 in beds, so 30.5 in between the inner
rows of adjacent beds ([Cotton Inc., Influence of Row Configuration](https://www.cottoninc.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Influence-Of-Row-Configuration.pdf), snippet).
Yield: Pioneer's 179 paired comparisons at 31 locations in 2010 "showed no statistical grain yield advantage"; Lauer's 37
university locations 1995 to 2005 averaged 2.4 bu/a less than 30 in, range -9 to +5 (Pioneer twin-row page); UNL's table
over 16 site-years averages -1.2% (G2216). So twin rows stay rare, and when they appear the two lines are 18 to 20 cm
apart with plants staggered (the diamond pattern in planting-practice).

**Europe.** UK: 75 cm "the vast majority" (KWS UK, in planting-practice). KWS's sowing-rate page builds its whole table
on "75 cm row width" with a 37.5 cm alternative ([KWS UK, sowing rate maize](https://www.kws.com/gb/en/consulting/sowing/sowing-maize/sowing-rate-maize.html), vendor).
Germany: a Sachsen-Anhalt state trial calls 75 cm the "ortsübliche" (locally usual) row width and found 45 cm rows cut
grain yield 11% ([LLG Sachsen-Anhalt, Produktionstechnik Körnermais](https://llg.sachsen-anhalt.de/fileadmin/Bibliothek/Politik_und_Verwaltung/MLU/LLFG/Dokumente/04_themen/oekol_lb/Produktionstechnik_Koernermais.pdf), snippet).
France: trade press treats "un interrang classique de 75 ou 80 centimètres" as the reference and says precision planters
are commonly offered at 70, 75 or 80 cm ([Réussir](https://www.reussir.fr/grandes-cultures/un-positionnement-de-la-graine-peser-au-dela-du-rendement), snippet;
[Chambre d'agriculture 82](https://agri82.chambre-agriculture.fr/fileadmin/user_upload/National/FAL_commun/publications/Occitanie/Semoir_distribution_monograine.pdf), snippet).
Italy: a Sardinian trial calls 70 cm "canonical" ([Journal of Agricultural Science, narrow rows in a Mediterranean environment](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-agricultural-science/article/narrow-rows-increase-maize-grain-yield-regardless-of-plant-density-in-a-mediterranean-environment/A5B1B079767697689E8343730506BB86), snippet);
a silage review says maize "is traditionally grown in widely spaced rows (0.65–0.75 m)" ([MDPI Agronomy 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4395/13/4/1117), snippet).
No national farm-practice survey for any EU country was found. Turkey: 70 cm (ministry brochures, in planting-practice).

**Brazil.** The historical spacing was 80 to 90 cm; with the soybean-then-safrinha-corn succession, spacing was "fixado
entre 45 e 50 centímetros" so the same planter serves both crops, and populations now run 60,000 to 80,000 plants/ha
([Aegro, espaçamento do milho](https://aegro.com.br/tags/espacamento-do-milho/), trade source). A UEMS text says planter
standardisation across soybean, corn, sorghum, bean and sunflower "fez com que muitos produtores rurais da região
Centro-Oeste utilizassem os espaçamentos de 45 ou 50 cm" ([UEMS via search](https://repositoriodigital.univag.com.br/index.php/agro/article/view/581/577), snippet).
Cruz et al. 2010 surveyed 1,138 high-yield safrinha fields (Centre-West, SP, PR) and found a mean population of about
55,000 plants/ha; the spacing distribution was not in the excerpt ([Embrapa, RBMS](https://rbms.sede.embrapa.br/ojs/article/download/980/pdf/9856), snippet).
Trials span 0.45 to 0.90 m. No acreage share by spacing was found; for the Centre-West assume 45 to 50 cm, for the
South 45 to 80 cm.

**Africa.** Malawi, 240 fields in six districts (full read): recommended 75 cm ridges by 25 cm in-row, one plant per
station, 53,333 plants/ha; observed ridge spacing "exceeded the recommended 75 cm distance in 90% of the sampled
fields" with district means 79.6 to 91.7 cm; in-row spacing 21 to 115 cm, over 80% of fields above 25 cm, most fields two
plants per station; fewer than 10% of fields reached the recommended population, district means 36,365 to 48,115
plants/ha; the traditional system was 90 x 90 cm with three seeds per station (about 37,000 plants/ha)
([Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2025.1629487/full)).
Kenya: a Keiyo North trial tested 75 x 20/25/30 cm and recommended 75 x 25 or 75 x 30 by site
([East African Journal of Agriculture and Biotechnology](https://journals.eanso.org/index.php/eajab/article/view/3415), snippet).
Nigeria: an Asaba trial recommended 75 x 15 cm and reported farmers using 90 x 30, 90 x 20, 90 x 50 and 75 x 50 cm
([ADSU Journal](https://adsujsr.adsu.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/VEGETATIVE-GROWTH-OF-MAIZE.pdf), snippet).
Zimbabwe dryland: yield risk rose below 90 cm rows and above 30,000 plants/ha (snippet). South Africa: Grain SA on-farm
trials use 0.5, 0.76, 0.91 and 1.0 m rows ([SA Grain, March 2017](https://grainsa.co.za/sagrain/march2017/files/assets/basic-html/page-98.html), snippet);
a North-West province study compared 0.52 and 0.76 m at 20,000 to 60,000 plants/ha
([Haarhoff and Swanepoel 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9214209/), snippet). The ultra-wide 1.5 and 2.1 m
dryland rows sometimes mentioned for the western Free State: not found. Takeaway for Africa: ridges or rows 75 to 90 cm,
with the in-row spacing and plants per station far more variable than anywhere else, two or three plants per station
common, so the "plant" from above is a clump.

**China.** The Northeast's conventional pattern is uniform 65 cm ridges ("65-cm row spacing is the conventional
uniform-row planting pattern for maize in Northeast China", [INCDA Fundulea, Romanian Agricultural Research 34](https://www.incda-fundulea.ro/rar/nr34/rar34.28.pdf), snippet;
Northeast trials use 65 cm uniform as the control, [Agriculture 2021](https://doaj.org/article/cfee27518e074b369e6a0575fbfaace2), snippet);
one survey found "more than four-fifths of farmers" on the 65 x 65 cm pattern (snippet, source not identified). The
Huang-Huai-Hai summer maize region treats 60 cm as the equal-row baseline ([Acta Agronomica Sinica 2016](https://zwxb.chinacrops.org/EN/Y2016/V42/I01/104), snippet);
wide-narrow pairs (80 + 40 cm, 100 + 20 cm, 40 + 160 cm) are the trialled alternatives. No national adoption figure.

**What this means for the row finder.** 76 cm in North America with 51 cm (20 in) and 56 cm (22 in) as the northern
alternatives and 91 to 97 cm (36 to 38 in) in the South; 70 to 80 cm in Europe and Turkey; 45 to 50 cm in central Brazil;
75 to 90 cm in Africa; 60 to 65 cm in China, with a paired 80/40 pattern possible. Twin rows at 18 to 20 cm on 76 cm
centres are rare but have a distinctive paired signature.

## 2. Plant population and in-row spacing

**Formula.** Rows per acre of row length: 43,560 ft² x 12 in/ft / row width (in) = inches of row per acre; so
in-row spacing (in) = 43,560 x 144 / (seeds per acre x row width in). Metric: spacing (cm) = 10,000 / (plants per ha x
row width m) x 100 / 100, i.e. spacing (m) = 1 / (plants per ha x row width m). Our arithmetic, seed spacing (plants
would be 5 to 10% wider, see emergence below):

KWS publishes the same arithmetic for 75 cm rows at 95% emergence: 6 plants/m² = 21.1 cm, 8 = 15.8 cm, 10 = 12.7 cm,
12 = 10.6 cm in-row (KWS UK, vendor). NC State's rule: never under 6 in in-row whatever the row width
([NC State, plant population and seed spacing](https://pasquotank.ces.ncsu.edu/2023/03/plant-population-and-seed-spacing-impact-on-corn-yield), snippet).

**Measured US populations (survey, not recommendation).** Nebraska 2012 USDA survey: harvest populations 29,000
plants/a irrigated and 21,850 rainfed; "harvest population is typically 5 to 10 percent below seeding rate", so
seeding rates of 30,450 to 31,900 irrigated and 22,943 to 24,035 rainfed; the share of Nebraska area above 30,000 doubled
to 28% between 2008 and 2012 (G2216, full read). No national or other-state ARMS seeding-rate average was found.

**Recommended rates by region.** Nebraska on-farm economic optima: irrigated 28,000 to 40,000 seeds/a, mean 34,000
(yields 206 to 251 bu/a); rainfed 24,000 to 30,000, mean 26,000 (yields 76 to 179 bu/a) (G2216). Iowa and Illinois:
36,000 plants = 37,800 to 39,600 seeds/a (G2216 citing those states); Illinois trials peak near 37,000 seeds (snippet);
Purdue: 28,000 final plants within 1 bu/a of 35,000, agronomic optimum about 34,000 seeds on normal soils
([Farm Progress, find optimum seeding rate](https://www.farmprogress.com/corn/find-optimum-corn-seeding-rate-for-your-farm), snippet).
Arkansas: 32,000 to 34,000 irrigated, 25,000 non-irrigated ([Arkansas corn quick facts 2024](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/farm-ranch/crops-commercial-horticulture/docs/2024-arkansas-corn-quick-facts.pdf), snippet).
Kentucky: 32,000 to 42,000 irrigated at 95% emergence ([UK grain crops, 2026](https://graincrops.mgcafe.uky.edu/articles/corn-populations-2026-kentucky), snippet);
grain 22,000 to 30,000 on the non-irrigated range (Lee, snippet). So the US range is roughly 20,000 (dryland Plains and
South) to 40,000 (irrigated and high-yield Corn Belt); in 30 in rows that is 10.5 in to 5.0 in (27 to 13 cm) seed
spacing.

**Silage.** Cornell: about 4,000 more kernels/a than grain, final stands about 3,500 higher, maximum dry matter near
40,000 plants/a ([Cornell NRCCA CA5](https://nrcca.cals.cornell.edu/crop/CA5/CA0523.php), snippet); Minnesota: final
stand 33,500 to 35,500 plants/a, rows narrower than 30 in "if possible", 15 or 20 in rows gave 7% more silage in
Wisconsin trials ([UMN, corn silage](https://extension.umn.edu/corn-planting/strategies-optimize-corn-silage-production), full read),
and elsewhere +2,000 seeds over grain with BMR hybrids slightly below grain rate (Dairy Herd, snippet); SDSU +10%
(snippet); Kentucky silage 24,000 to 30,000 (snippet). Sources disagree on the increment (+2,000 to +4,000) but agree
on direction. From above, silage corn is grain corn 5 to 10% denser and more often in 20 in rows.

**Sweet corn.** Fresh market (Purdue): rows 30 to 40 in, in-row 8 to 10 in early and 9 to 12 in late varieties, 13,068
to 26,136 plants/a ([Purdue fruit and vegetable trials](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/fvtrials/5), snippet). Processing
(full read): 30 on-farm fields in Illinois, Minnesota and Wisconsin 2013 to 2017; "the average current plant density is
56,000 plants ha-1" (22,700/a); optimum ranged 60,850 to 90,900 plants/ha; mean optimum 76,000 irrigated and 70,700
rainfed; row spacing not stated ([Dhaliwal and Williams 2020, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7006923/)).
Georgia shipper sweet corn is on beds with two or three rows per bed top and 15 to 25 cm in-row, 43,000 to 107,000
plants/ha ([ASHS 2024 abstract](https://ashs2024.sched.com/event/1dqWv/vcm-6-optimizing-planting-density-of-sweet-corn-in-georgia), snippet).
So sweet corn is sparser than field corn (about 60% of its density in the Midwest) and in the South sits on beds, a
nested lattice like California vegetables.

**Seed corn.** Fields are planted in repeating panels of female and male inbred rows; "the common ratios are six
female rows with two male rows, four female rows with one male row", with 4:2 and 4:1:6:1 less common
([Wikipedia, Detasseling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detasseling), snippet); Pioneer: "we plant one row of the male
inbreds, then four rows of the females" (snippet); a patent lists 2:2, 3:2, 4:1, 4:2, 4:3, 6:1 and 6:2
([USPTO 11632921](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11632921), snippet). Rows stay on the
field's normal spacing: Culy, Edwards and Cornelius 1991 measured yield by "each additional 30 in. row distance from the
pollinator" (about 550,000 seeds/a less per row away) ([Crop Science 1991 via AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65df867eb766d82b18021c6f), snippet).
Female rows are detasseled (machine then hand crews), and the male rows are chopped or cut out after pollination so
they do not contaminate the harvest ([FarmShow chopper](https://www.farmshow.com/a_article.php?aid=985), snippet;
[Canadian certification standard](https://seedgrowers.ca/wp-content/uploads/pdf/en/CORN-HYBRID-FOUNDATION-CERTIFIED.pdf), snippet).
Female population per acre: not found. Male rows are often planted on split dates to spread pollen (patent, snippet). From
above, a seed field in July shows a strict 4:1 or 6:2 periodicity of taller (tasselled male) and shorter, cut-topped
female rows, and from August an empty row every fifth or every seventh to eighth row where the males were destroyed.
This is also where interseeded cover crops are most common (see section 6).

**Organic and non-GM.** Cornell's New York trials: organic corn at about 30,000 kernels/a ended at 24,500 to 25,500
plants/a by V3, judged too low; high-input organic at about 35,000 kernels reached 27,000 to 29,000; the authors
suggest about 33,000 to 34,000 kernels/a; a rotary hoe pass plus three in-row cultivations cut the stand a further
3.5% (about 1,000 plants/a), most of it from soil thrown onto small plants
([Cornell What's Cropping Up, 2017](https://blogs.cornell.edu/whatscroppingup/2017/07/26/close-cultivation-followed-by-three-in-row-cultivations-reduce-organic-corn-plant-densities-by-another-3-5-or-1000-plantsacre), snippet).
A SARE project (LNC25-524) states there is "virtually no work" on organic corn seeding rates (snippet). Row spacing in
organic corn is the same 30 in (cultivator-driven); nothing suggests otherwise. Non-GM conventional corn: no separate
seeding guidance found; treated as conventional.

**Emergence and the plant count.** Warm germination of seed corn "typically ranges from 90 to 95 percent" and field
emergence is "90 - 95 % of the seeding rate" ([Purdue AGRY-91-01](https://agry.purdue.edu/Ext/corn/pubs/agry9101.htm), full read);
Ohio State: 5 to 10% of seeds fail even without damage (snippet); KWS assumes 95% (vendor). So the plant lattice has a
5 to 10% random dropout before any planter error.

## 3. Planting precision and irregularity

**Plant-spacing standard deviation (the headline numbers).** Purdue, 354 commercial fields in Indiana and Ohio,
1987 to 1996: "standard deviation of plant spacing was three inches or less in only about 16% of the fields", "about
60% of the sampled fields exhibited standard deviations of plant spacing between four and five inches", about 24% at
6 in or more, values "as great as 12 inches"; mean about 4.5 in; the target is "about 2 inches (5 cm)" because
non-emergence alone spreads plant spacing even when seed spacing is perfect (AGRY-91-01 full read;
[Nielsen 2004 update](https://agry.purdue.edu/Ext/corn/research/psv/Update2004.html), full read). Wisconsin, 127 fields
1998 to 2000: mean SD 3.3 in, range 1.9 to 6.8 in, 95% of fields under 4.6 in (Lauer and Rankin 2004 via Nielsen 2004).
Ontario trials deliberately set SDs of 6.7 to 16.2 cm (2.6 to 6.4 in)
([Liu et al. 2004, Agronomy Journal, via AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65de3e917c7033e84bea3bb3), snippet).
Nielsen's paired fields: both 8.7 in mean spacing at about 24,000 plants/a, SDs 6.8 versus 3.5 in. Yield: Nielsen 2.5
bu/a per inch of SD (1.2 to 4.5 across site-years, 1987 to 1993), 2.2 in the 2004 trial, Pioneer (Doerge 2002) 3.4;
Lauer and Rankin (Wisconsin) and Liu et al. (Ontario) found no significant yield effect within the commercial range.
That disagreement is about yield, not about the geometry; all sources agree commercial corn has an in-row SD of roughly
3 to 5 in (8 to 13 cm) on a 6 to 9 in (15 to 23 cm) mean, i.e. a coefficient of variation of about 40 to 70% (our
arithmetic). The in-row lattice in a real field is therefore weak; the row line is the reliable structure.

**Skips and doubles (ISO-style indices).** Definitions from Kachman and Smith 1995 (Transactions ASAE 38(2):379-387):
multiples index D = share of spacings at or below half the nominal; miss index M = share above 1.5 times nominal;
quality of feed A = the rest; precision C = CV of the remaining spacings, practical upper limit 29%
([Iowa State, plant uniformity measurements](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/plant-uniformity-measurements), full read).
Measured, University of Nebraska 2001, 15 on-farm sites, plant (not seed) spacings:

| Planter speed | D doubles | M skips | A quality | C precision | Yield bu/a |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 mph | 4.9% | 10.7% | 84.4% | 18.7% | 207.1 |
| 4 mph | 5.0% | 10.5% | 84.5% | 20.1% | 205.6 |
| 6 mph | 7.6% | 12.6% | 79.9% | 23.1% | 205.5 |
| 15-site mean (range) | 5.8 (1.5 to 10.6) | 11.3 (4.9 to 18.4) | 82.9 (71.0 to 88.1) | 20.6 (16.4 to 26.1) | 206 (187 to 235) |

Source: [Iowa State, planter speed and plant spacing](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/planter-speed-affect-plant-spacing), full read.
These are plant-level, so the 11% skips include the 5 to 10% non-emergence. Seed-level: a university test of a modern
planter gave 0.35% doubles and 1.21% skips (Väderstad, in planting-practice); Precision Planting says growers without
monitors "may have only obtained 95% singulation" and modern planters reach 99.5% (vendor); a Purdue farm monitor read 96%
with some seed lots and 98 to 99% after adjustment ([Farm Progress](https://www.farmprogress.com/corn/seed-singulation-matters-more-than-planter-speed), snippet).
Yield cost per percentage point of singulation lost: 1.9 to 2.2 bu/a (Precision Planting, vendor), 0.8 (AGCO, vendor).
A Brazilian 50 cm trial read skips from control charts: with the free SF1 signal at 5 km/h the median miss rate was
about 25% with an interquartile range to 70%, with SF2 near zero; acceptable spacings about 85% (SF1) and 95% (SF2) at
5 km/h, near 100% for both at 8 km/h, which the authors did not fully explain
([Souza et al. 2026, Acta Scientiarum Agronomy 48](https://doi.org/10.4025/actasciagron.v48i1.73913), full read; treat
the speed result as odd). Causes: worn finger-pickup parts, finger tension, knock-off brushes, air pressure, disc size,
speed; double drops land 1 to 2 in apart (Nielsen 2004).

**Emergence uniformity.** Rule of thumb: leaf stages apart x 3 = days apart ([Iowa State, uneven corn heights](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/uneven-corn-heights), full read).
Trials (same source plus [Illinois, Hill and Furrow 2014](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/hill-and-furrow/2014-05-19-uneven-corn-emergence-and-yield-potential), full read):
Illinois/Wisconsin (Nafziger et al. 1991): a 10-day delay cost 6 to 7% (6 to 9% in the Illinois write-up) regardless of
the fraction delayed; a 21-day delay cost 10% (25% of plants late), 20% (50%) and 23% (75%) (Carter et al. 1989 gives
20 to 22% for 50 to 75%). Ontario (Liu et al. 2004, Crop Science 44:847): one plant in six delayed two leaf stages -4%,
four leaf stages -8%; plants beside a gap compensate, plants beside a late emerger do not; corn "is more responsive to
plant emergence variability than plant spacing variability" ([AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65df36027c7033e84bed1448), full abstract).
Minnesota (Ford and Hicks): 7 versus 14 days, the penalty grows with population. Ohio State: replanting gains under 5% if
the delay is under 2 weeks, up to 10% if half or more are 3 weeks late; late plants under a quarter of the stand do not
matter ([OSU AGF-122](https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/agf-122), full read). Agronomists' target is emergence within 24
hours, at worst 48 ([Farm Progress](https://www.farmprogress.com/corn/why-agronomists-preach-uniform-corn-emergence), snippet);
Iowa State calls stands problematic when early and late plants are more than 2 weeks apart (snippet). Causes: soil
temperature, seeding depth, residue distribution, crusting, moisture (ISU). No survey of how common late emergers are
in commercial fields was found; the Illinois/Wisconsin trials used 25 to 75% delayed as treatments, not observations.

**Stand loss after emergence.** Insects, frost, hail, flooding, poor seedbed (OSU, snippet); cultivation in organic
systems about 3.5% (Cornell, snippet); yield held to a 20% stand reduction in a Bayer trial, significant loss at 30%
(vendor, snippet).

**How these look from above (our inference, at 1 to 3 cm per pixel).** Target in-row spacing is 14 to 20 cm in 30 in
rows. A skip by definition is a gap over 1.5 times nominal, so over 21 to 30 cm; with 11% of spacings being skips there
is one visible gap every 9 spacings on average, about every 1.5 to 2 m of row, and a quarter of fields have SDs that
make gaps of 30 to 45 cm ordinary. Doubles are two seeds 2.5 to 5 cm apart, which at V3 to V5 is one blob with a
slightly larger canopy, not two plants. A late emerger is a plant a leaf stage or more behind, visibly smaller for the
whole vegetative season, with no gap beside it. Non-emergence (5 to 10%) is indistinguishable from a planter skip. A
"gap" in a corn row is therefore ordinary and carries no weed information on its own; what does carry information is a
gap that holds a green object the row's plants do not explain. This is the basis for the no-red-crosses rule in
planting-layout: a missing plant is the operator's word, not the imagery's.

## 4. Planter and field geometry

**Planter widths.** Pass width = rows x spacing. John Deere 1775NT field operation widths from dealer specs: 12-row 30
in 9,937 mm (32.6 ft), 16-row 12,870 mm (42.2 ft), 24-row 18,977 mm (62.3 ft)
([Koenig Equipment listings](https://koenigequipment.com/agricultural-solutions/john-deere/products/john-deere-1775nt-12row30-drawn-planter), vendor);
DB series: DB60 24-row 30 in on a 60 ft toolbar ([Deere](https://deere.com/en/planting-equipment/db60-24row-split-48-planter)),
DB80 32-row 30 in on 80 ft ([Deere](https://deere.com/en/planting-equipment/db80-32row30-planter/)), DB90 36-row 30 in and
54-row 20 in, DB88 48-row 22 in, DB44 24-row 22 in ([Koenig brochure](https://info.koenigequipment.com/product-brochures/planting-equipment-brochure), vendor),
DB120 48-row 30 in on 120 ft ([Deere Canada](https://www.deere.ca/en/planting-equipment/db120-48row30-planter/), vendor);
1795 split-row 12/23-24 and 16/31-32 (15/30 in) and 24-row 20 in. Eight-row 30 in (20 ft) planters exist but no spec page
was reached this session. So the planter pass in Corn Belt imagery is most often 12, 16 or 24 rows (9.1, 12.2, 18.3 m
at 30 in), with 36 and 48 rows (27.4, 36.6 m) on large farms and 6 or 8 rows on small ones; in 20 in rows 24-row (12.2
m) and 54-row (27.4 m).

**The guess row.** Defined in a patent: "the row space between the outside rows of adjacent planter passes. Guess row
spacing is dependent on driving accuracy relative to the adjacent pass", while all other spacings are fixed by the frame
([US 2011/0259618](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20110259618)). Its error is the pass-to-pass error of the guidance:
John Deere StarFire 6000, 95% over 15 minutes: SF1 +/-15 cm, SF2 +/-5 cm (dealer table), SF3 +/-3 cm, Radio RTK and
Mobile RTK +/-2.5 cm ([Deere Mobile RTK page](https://www.deere.ca/en/technology-products/precision-ag-technology/guidance/mobile-rtk/);
[Sloan Implement StarFire 6000 spec](https://www.sloans.com/catalog/new-john-deere-ag/precision-equipment/receivers/starfire-6000-with-sf3/print), vendor).
Measured in 24 farmer fields in Çukurova, Turkey, cotton and corn set at 70 to 75 cm: mean pass-to-pass inter-row
deviation 7.4 cm manual, 5.0 cm CORS, 5.9 cm SBAS, 6.7 cm RTK, so even RTK farms ran 6 to 7 cm because of implement
offset and settings ([Topçueri, Keskin and Şekerli 2024, Journal of Tekirdağ Agricultural Faculty 21(1)](https://doi.org/10.33462/jotaf.1231452), full read of the abstract page).
Precision Planting's header tests: manual steering SD 1.9 in (4.8 cm) from row centre, automatic 0.5 to 1 in; 1 in off
costs 0.3 bu/a, 2 in 0.7, over 3 to 5 in more than 5 bu/a of header loss
([Precision Planting TrueSight report](https://www.precisionplanting.com/resources/research/truesight-harvest-loss-report), vendor).
A patent notes guidance may not meet the "+/- 4 inch" the corn head needs ([US 8010261](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8010261)).
Deere markets AutoTrac Implement Guidance as making "every row – including guess rows" the same spacing (vendor claim).
Practical reading: expect the guess row every 12, 16 or 24 rows to be off by 5 to 15 cm (up to 23 cm on an old free
signal), occasionally more where the planter drifted on a slope; within a pass the pitch is exact to the frame.

**Headlands, end rows, point rows.** A headland (turnrow) is "the area at each end of a planted field", planted with
rows "perpendicular to the lay of the field" and "usually two, three or four times the width of the implement used for
planting" ([Wikipedia, Headland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headland_(agriculture))). Farmers say at least 12 rows or
two planter widths, 16 for large combines; some plant them first (to know where to lift the planter), some last (less
compaction) ([Yesterday's Tractors forum](https://forums.yesterdaystractors.com/threads/headlands.1000570/), forum).
Headland soil is more compacted and yields less (Wikipedia). Point rows are the partial rows that meet a boundary at
an angle; "most fields include many point rows because of wetlands and potholes" even when they look rectangular
([NDSU 2010](https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/news/newsreleases/2010/may-24-2010/producers-may-want-to-consider-section-and-row-control-for-planters), snippet);
double-planted point-row area ranged 0.1 to 20% of the field in a Tennessee study, a diagonal-split quarter section
planted with a 12-row planter overlaps nearly 2 acres, and Alabama section control saved 4.3% on average
([No-Till Farmer](https://www.no-tillfarmer.com/articles/1107-look-at-section-row-control-for-planters), snippet;
[NCC 2011 abstract](https://ncc.confex.com/ncc/2011/webprogram/Paper11591.html), snippet). From above: a band 2 to 4
planter widths deep at each end with rows at right angles to the body of the field; a triangle of rows shortening one
by one against any diagonal edge; doubled density in the overlap wedge where no section control was used.

**Contours, terraces, pivots.** Current shares of corn acres on contours or terraces: not found. Iowa's inventory
counts 506,100 terraces, 557,700 acres of contour buffer strips and 109,800 acres of strip cropping on about 30.6
million farmed acres ([Wildlife Management Institute on the Iowa BMP mapping project](https://wildlifemanagement.institute/node/920), snippet);
the 1969 Census had 9% of commercial farms contouring row crops (snippet). Centre pivots: a 160-acre circle in a quarter
section leaves 25 acres of dry corners ([Wikipedia, Center-pivot irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center-pivot_irrigation), snippet);
rows are straight across the circle unless the pivot runs LEPA drops, where "corn is typically planted in a circular
pattern to accommodate the drop pipes" and furrow dikes are built in the circular row middles
([UGA Bulletin 882](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/B-882_5.pdf), snippet; the PDF would not
parse). No figure for the share of pivot corn planted in circles was found; it is a Plains and Southeast LEPA practice.

**Row direction.** No convention was documented. Farmers "plant down the long side" for the longest rows (forum). Trials
disagree on yield: North Dakota found north-south rows 12 bu/a below east-west, 28 bu/a below in 30 in rows
([NDSU Carrington](https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/greenhouse/carringtonrec/center-points/a-sneak-peek-at-2020-wide-row-corn-results), snippet);
an Iranian trial favoured north-south (snippet); a 1957 study found stands between north-south rows more uniform
(snippet). For the row finder this means: do not assume an orientation; take it from the field's long axis or the
boundary, and expect it to change at headlands.

**Tramlines and wheel tracks.** Corn has no tramline system; sprayer and side-dress wheels run in the row middles on
the 30 in pitch, and a 120 ft boom spans 48 rows. No source describing this was found this session (searches went to
planters); stated here as unsourced field practice. In furrow-irrigated corn the tractor-track furrows are the "hard"
furrows and the others "soft" (UNL G1720, section 6), which is visible as alternating wet and dry middles.

**Row straightness and parallelism, summarised.** Within a pass: pitch exact, rows parallel. Between passes: a step of
5 to 15 cm at the guess row, same sign for the length of the pass. Along a pass: straight to GNSS accuracy (centimetres
per 100 m) on autosteer; curved on contours, terraces and LEPA circles; a 2 to 4 in (5 to 10 cm) wobble with manual
steering (Precision Planting SD 1.9 in).

## 5. Tillage and residue

**Shares (USDA ARMS via ERS).** Corn no-till: 16% (2001), 28% (2016), 36% (2021); conservation tillage (no-till plus
mulch-till) on 76% of corn acres, mulch-till "about half of conservation tillage acres on corn"
([ERS chart of note 105042](https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/105042), full read; the 76% carries no
year on the page, by context 2021). EIB-197 for 2016 corn land: no-till or strip-till used continuously 2013 to 2016 on
18%, alternated with full-width tillage on 27%, full-width tillage continuously on 55%; no-till or strip-till on 27% of
2016 corn acres; conservation tillage 65% of 2016 corn
([ERS chart of note 92981](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/92981), full read;
[Farm Policy News on EIB-197](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/?p=9065), snippet). Strip-till alone: ERS folds it
into no-till; No-Till Farmer estimated 7.7% of cropland strip-tilled in 2014 and 3.6 million corn acres in 2007
(trade, snippets). Our reading: by 2021 roughly a third of corn is no-till (half of that strip-till at most), a third
mulch-till (chisel or disk leaving over 30% residue), a quarter to a third conventionally tilled.

**Rotation.** ARMS 2010: "In 2010, half of U.S. corn producers planted soy[beans in rotation]", tabulated as "Corn –
soybean rotation (50% of farms)" ([ERS ERR-217, 2016](https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/80326/ERR-217.pdf),
full text, farms not acres). Acre-based: Illinois CDL 1999 to 2003, 62 to 66% of corn acres followed soybeans
([NASS table via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2396611/table/T3), snippet); corn's share of corn-plus-soybean
acres 2001 to 2006 was 63% Nebraska, 54% Iowa, 53% Illinois, "Nebraska ~2:1, Iowa/Illinois ~1:1"
([NASS, Craig 2007](https://data-origin.nass.usda.gov/Education_and_Outreach/Reports,_Presentations_and_Conferences/Presentations/Craig_WAOB07_CropRotation.pdf), snippet);
Iowa State estimated about a third of Iowa corn as corn-on-corn in 2011 (snippet); Illinois corn share fell from about
47% (2012) to 40% (2024) of farm acres ([farmdoc daily 2026](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/06/historical-corn-versus-soybean-returns-in-illinois.html), snippet).
A national 2016 or 2021 acre share of corn following soybeans was not found. Working assumption: 60 to 70% of Corn Belt
corn follows soybeans, 25 to 35% follows corn, more corn-on-corn in Nebraska and northern Illinois and under irrigation.

**What residue looks like.** NRCS conservation compliance is at least 30% residue cover after planting; "strict
no-till systems can maintain as much as 60 percent cover from corn and 40 percent from soybeans"
([MSU Extension, measure residue cover](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/measure_residue_cover_to_protect_the_soil), snippet);
soybean residue "breaks down quicker ... provides less residue cover initially" and 30% after fall tillage of soybean
ground is "highly unlikely" ([UW Hendrickson 2002](https://extension.soils.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/68/2016/07/Hendrickson-Conf-2002.pdf), snippet).
Remote sensing: residue is classed above 30% as conservation tillage, usually assessed right after planting; a drone
study reached R² 0.79 against line-transect cover and was more reliable in no-till than tilled fields; soil moisture and
emerging crop confound the estimate ([Remote Sensing 2024, 16:1135](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/7/1135/htm), snippet).
Our inference for the imagery, with no source describing the visual pattern: no-till corn after corn shows bright
stalk and leaf straw with last year's stalk rows still legible, usually offset about 15 in from the new rows because
growers plant between old rows; no-till corn after soybeans shows a thin grey stubble with faint old 30 in (or 15 in)
drill lines and much bare, dark soil; strip-till shows a dark tilled band, typically 8 to 10 in wide (common strip
widths, not sourced this session), centred on each new row with straw between; mulch-till shows chopped straw mixed
through brown soil without row structure; conventional tillage is bare soil. Residue stripes are a false row signal for
a row finder: they run on the same pitch as the crop, at a phase of 0 or half a pitch.

## 6. Cover crops, intercrops and furrows

**Interseeded cover crops.** The window is V4 to V6: drilled at V3 or earlier covers cut Pennsylvania corn yield 8 to
20% at one site, at V5 to V6 they did not; South Dakota found no loss at V5
([USU Extension, interseeding in the Intermountain West](https://extension.usu.edu/crops/research/interseeding-cover-crops-into-corn-in-the-intermountain-west), snippet;
[NRCS, Interseeding Cover Crops into Corn](https://nrcs-prod.azureedge.us/sites/default/files/2022-09/InterseedingCCIntoCorn.pdf), snippet).
Equipment: high-clearance toolbars with one to three drill units between each pair of rows; a New York agronomist seeded
750 acres with a 12-row toolbar in late June 2015 with radish, crimson clover and annual ryegrass
([SARE, Roth, establishment methods](https://www.sare.org/wp-content/uploads/Establishment-Methods-for-Cover-Crops_Roth.pdf), snippet).
Herbicide residuals are the main constraint. Adoption share of corn acres: not found; a Nebraska survey of 82
respondents found interseeding "the main establishment method in seed corn" (because seed corn is harvested early and
the canopy is thin after male-row removal) ([UNL CropWatch 2017](https://cropwatch.unl.edu/2017/cover-crop-management-among-nebraska-farm-group), snippet).
Wide-row (60 in) corn with interseeded covers is trialled in North Dakota and Pennsylvania ([Farm Progress 2019](https://www.farmprogress.com/cover-crops/interseeding-cover-crops-in-extra-wide-corn-rows), snippet).
From above an interseeded field shows one to three fine green lines in every alley from about V6, which a cover-mask
grader would count as weeds unless told.

**Relay and strip intercropping.** US: alternating 20 ft corn and soybean strips (8 rows at 30 in) are trialled by
Precision Planting and found most profitable east-west over 2021 to 2023 (vendor, snippet). China: maize-soybean strip
systems at 2:2, 4:4, 4:6 and 3:4 row ratios, maize narrow rows of 40 cm inside wide pairs
([Scientia Agricultura Sinica 2025](https://www.chinaagrisci.com/EN/Y2025/V58/I23/4858), snippet;
[PMC 7290029](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7290029/), snippet). Africa: maize-bean intercrop
"predominant in eastern Africa", maize with cowpea, groundnut and bambara in southern Africa, "widespread" with no area
share found ([ILRI/CGIAR review](https://dspace7test.ilri.org/items/f092f93c-d5d0-44cb-bc16-33041a9a9b45/full), snippet).
For the row finder: two interleaved lattices, the legume in the alley at its own pitch, or alternating blocks of 4 to 8
rows.

**Furrow-irrigated corn.** "Approximately 40 percent of the irrigated acres in Nebraska are furrow-irrigated"; furrows
run on the row pitch, either every furrow or "every other row in a 12-row planting and cultivation system"; tractor
wheel traffic compacts some furrows into "hard" furrows that take water faster, the rest are "soft"
([UNL G1720, furrow irrigation management](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1720/na/html/view), full read).
Alternate-furrow versus every-furrow gave the same corn response in Colorado for the same water (snippet); Idaho
compared 30 in rows with offset 22 in pairs under furrows (snippet); Arizona and LSU use 40 in beds with twin rows for
cotton and corn in the South (snippets). From above: ridged rows with a dark, wet furrow between every row or every
second row during an irrigation set, straight and long (runs of a quarter mile are ordinary; no length figure sourced),
and bare furrow bottoms that never carry weeds the way a flat alley does.

## Gaps and uncertainties

- No US row-width survey newer than Pioneer 2015; USDA-NASS has farmer-reported row widths 2013 to 2017 but only as a
  vendor map. No national ARMS seeding-rate or row-width table found.
- Twin-row share rests on a vendor "under 1%" and a snippet "under 0.2% of 2009".
- EU, Brazil, Africa and China spacings come from trial baselines and trade text, not national practice surveys;
  Malawi (240 fields) is the one measured distribution.
- Seed-corn female population per acre and the row pitch of male rows: not found.
- Organic seeding rates: one Cornell trial series; SARE says the literature is empty.
- The frequency of late emergers in commercial fields: not found (trials impose 17 to 75%).
- Guess-row error measured on corn planters in North America: not found; the Turkish and Brazilian measurements and
  Deere's receiver specs stand in.
- Headland depth, point-row area and the share of fields with them: forum and section-control snippets only.
- Share of corn on contours or terraces, and of pivot corn planted in circles: not found.
- Row direction convention: not found; three trials disagree on yield.
- Strip-till share of corn acres on its own: ERS folds it into no-till; trade estimates 2007 and 2014 only.
- National acre share of corn following soybeans for 2016 or 2021: not found; ERR-217 gives farms (50%) for 2010.
- Residue cover after planting by previous crop and tillage (the Purdue AY-313 table) could not be fetched; MSU's 60 /
  40% figures are a snippet. The visual description of residue stripes and strip-till bands is our own, unsourced.
- Interseeded cover crop adoption share: not found; SARE/CTIC survey figure exists but was unreadable.
- Furrow length and bed widths for furrow corn in Nebraska, Texas and Kansas: not found beyond the 40% figure.
- Web-search budget ran out before: Purdue NCH-36 table (certificate error), UGA B-882 circle planting (PDF unparsed),
  Deere planter catalogue page (empty), AY-313 residue table, corn after soybean in EIB-248 (searched the text, absent).
