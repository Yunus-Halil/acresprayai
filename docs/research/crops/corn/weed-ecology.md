# Corn: weed ecology by region and season, and what looks like what from above

Research of 2026-10-23. Extends section D of [../corn.md](../corn.md) (which already covers waterhemp and giant
ragweed escapes, the Peña 2013 row-position result, the 0.3 to 0.5 cm species classifiers, and resistance
counts for waterhemp and Palmer amaranth) and the catalog rules in
[../../../features/weed-catalog.md](../../../features/weed-catalog.md). Nothing here is an identification rule:
a species being common in a region and season raises a prior, it never names a spot.

Conventions. "(snippet)" means the figure was seen in a search result or an abstract and the full source was not
read. "(not retrieved)" means the source is known to exist but could not be opened this session (wssa.net,

## 1. Who grows where: the survey evidence

### 1.1 The WSSA surveys that include corn

WSSA runs a three-year rotation: broadleaf crops (2019, 2022, 2025), grass crops including corn (2020, 2023),
aquatic and non-crop (2021, 2024). The task brief's "2016/2019/2022" rounds are broadleaf-crop rounds and do not
contain corn; the corn rounds are the grass-crop ones.

**2020 grass-crop survey** (Van Wychen and Hand, WSSA 2021 annual meeting abstract 34, read from the
[WSSA 2021 abstracts PDF](https://wssa.net/wp-content/uploads/WSSA-Virtual-Annual-Meeting-all-abstracts-updated-4-12-2021ab_FINAL.pdf)).
317 responses from weed scientists and affiliates in the US and Canada. "Common weeds refer to the weeds you most
frequently see while troublesome weeds are the most difficult to control, but might not be widespread."

| Corn, 2020 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 to 6 (vendor summary, snippet) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Most common | common lambsquarters (*Chenopodium album*) | *Setaria* spp. (foxtails) | waterhemp (*Amaranthus tuberculatus*) | pigweed (redroot/smooth), Palmer amaranth, morningglory |
| Most troublesome | Palmer amaranth (*A. palmeri*), by one vote | waterhemp | *Ipomoea* spp. (morningglory) | giant ragweed, common lambsquarters, johnsongrass, kochia |

([Worst weeds in corn](https://www.corteva.com/us/Resources/crop-protection/corn/worst-weeds-corn.html), vendor
page; it says "top six" but names seven troublesome weeds). The same page says that against the previous grass-crop
round velvetleaf and common ragweed dropped out of the common top six and foxtail out of the troublesome top six.
Evidence strength: expert-opinion survey, continental, respondent base 317; not field counts.

### 1.2 The regional picture from state and society sources

**Corn Belt.** The 2020 WSSA lists above are dominated by Corn Belt respondents. The only in-field survey found
is Wisconsin 2012, 151 fields: common lambsquarters first, foxtails and velvetleaf near the top
([Recker, UW slides](https://extension.soils.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/68/2016/07/Recker-2.pdf), snippet,
file too large to fetch). Giant ragweed is the Corn Belt escape (29% of 1,300 Indiana fields, GWC-12, in corn.md).
Shattercane and woolly cupgrass appear in the Missouri, Wisconsin and Virginia keys as Corn Belt grasses.

**Northern Plains.** No corn-specific survey found. NDSU's 2000 survey was of small-grain stubble (green foxtail,
wild buckwheat, kochia, lambsquarters at the top; waterhemp in four counties, now spreading) and its current
resistant-weed list is kochia, waterhemp, common ragweed, horseweed, wild oat, green foxtail
([NDSU weeds hub](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/ag-hub/ag-topics/crop-production/diseases-pests-and-weeds/weeds), snippet).
Kochia's seventh place in the WSSA troublesome list is the Plains signal.

**Southern Plains.** Texas: Palmer amaranth "infests all cultivated cropland", "emerges from May–September"
([Keeling, Texas A&M AgriLife 2015 slides](https://legacy.research.agrilife.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2015/03/Weed-Management-Strategies-for-2015.pdf), read; cotton-centred).
Kansas: K-State corn trials target Palmer amaranth first, then kochia, velvetleaf, shattercane and green foxtail;
a 2014 K-State talk calls Palmer amaranth the most common weed of the central and southern Great Plains (snippets).
Texas trials name fall panicum, Texas millet and browntop panicum as the annual grasses (snippets).

**Southeast.** Webster and Nichols 2012 analysed the Southern Weed Science Society's annual surveys over 14
southern states: the top five corn weeds in both 1994 and 2009 were "morningglories, Texas millet, broadleaf
signalgrass, johnsongrass, and sicklepod"; "Palmer amaranth, smartweeds, and goosegrass had the greatest increases
in importance in corn"; in corn and wheat "12 of the 15 most troublesome weeds were the same in 1994 and 2008"
([Weed Science 60:145-157](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/changes-in-the-prevalence-of-weed-species-in-the-major-agronomic-crops-of-the-southern-united-states-19941995-to-20082009/1159683C705C38A3A860B41B82B377BC), abstract read).
Georgia's 1998 county-agent survey: "Texas panicum was the most troublesome weed in corn", sicklepod in the top
four, perennial nutsedge and pigweeds in the top five
([Webster and MacDonald 2001, Weed Technology 15:771](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-technology/article/abs/survey-of-weeds-in-various-crops-in-georgia/A1C140C8A3B12A461158C2FE2BB9B2DD), abstract read).
The current UGA guide: "The top 10 most troublesome weeds in Georgia field corn are as follows: 1) Texas panicum;
2) crabgrass; 3) morningglory species; 4) pigweed species; 5) sicklepod; 6) nutsedge species; 7) johnsongrass;
8) annual ryegrass; 9) Pennsylvania smartweed; and 10) Benghal dayflower"
([Georgia Corn Production Guide 2023](https://site.extension.uga.edu/laurens/files/2023/02/2023-Corn-Production-Guide.pdf), read).

**Mid-Atlantic including Virginia.** The Virginia Field Crops PMG corn chapter (Flessner, Singh et al.;
[2021 edition PDF](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/8a979bf1-2f21-4a62-ba14-b97fdcedbcc0/download), read)
rates post-emergence corn herbicides against: grasses barnyardgrass, bermudagrass, broadleaf signalgrass,
crabgrass, fall panicum, foxtail spp., goosegrass, johnsongrass (seedling and rhizome), quackgrass, shattercane,
Texas panicum, wirestem muhly, yellow nutsedge; broadleaves burcucumber, cocklebur, jimsonweed, lambsquarters,
marestail/horseweed, annual morningglory, eastern black nightshade, Palmer amaranth/waterhemp, pigweed, common
ragweed, giant ragweed, prickly sida, smartweed, spurred anoda, velvetleaf. That is the regional species list;
it is not ranked. The catalog's sixteen Virginia corn names (weed-catalog.md) are a subset.

**Northeast.** Cornell, May 2024: common NY field-corn annuals "redroot pigweed, Powell amaranth, common
lambsquarters, common ragweed, horseweed, common waterhemp, velvetleaf", plus "foxtails (yellow, green, and
giant), fall panicum"; perennials "field bindweeds, horsenettle, milkweed, yellow nutsedge, Canada thistle, hemp
dogbane, quackgrass" ([Kumar, Hunter, Stanyard](https://blogs.cornell.edu/whatscroppingup/?p=2782), read).
Pennsylvania: burcucumber and lambsquarters persistent, crabgrasses and yellow foxtail increasing (Curran 2004,
snippet, dated).

## 3. When they come up: emergence relative to corn

Corn is planted into soil at about 10 °C and emerges at about 100 to 120 GDD (°F base 50) (corn.md, section B).
The weeds are sequenced by their own thermal thresholds, and the order matters for what a flight at V2 to V5 sees.

**Early (with or before the crop).** Giant ragweed: "typically emerges early in the growing season, as early as
March, and some populations have adapted to extend emergence dates into midsummer"
([Take Action giant ragweed factsheet 2025](https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Updated-Giant-Ragweed-TA-Factsheet-2025.pdf), read).
In Iowa it "usually initiates emergence in early April, typically 2 to 4 weeks earlier than giant foxtail"; Iowa
biotypes reached 50% emergence within a week and 90% in under 20 days, while Ohio and Illinois agricultural
biotypes took 6 to 7 weeks to 50%, "very similar to how waterhemp behaves in Iowa"
([Iowa State, giant ragweed emergence patterns](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/giant-ragweed-emergence-patterns), read).
Indiana 2023 field data: first emergence at about 120 GDD (base 48 °F), peak 200 to 300
([Purdue Pest and Crop, Pearson](https://extension.entm.purdue.edu/newsletters/pestandcrop/article/midwestern-regional-climate-center-launches-new-experimental-weed-emergence-scouting-tool), read).
Common ragweed: Mid-Atlantic peak 7 April to 1 May, Nebraska 28 April, Ontario 90% before 15 June, and
"emergence was observed to curtail sharply in June". Common lambsquarters: Minnesota late April, Mid-Atlantic
11 May to 1 June, New York mid May, Wisconsin 11 June, "can be somewhat protracted". Velvetleaf started one week
after giant ragweed in a 1998 Iowa trial (ISU farm report, snippet). Kochia emergence timing: not retrieved.

**With the crop.** Giant foxtail: Mid-Atlantic peak 2 to 24 May, New York 28 April, Ohio 17 May, Wisconsin 8 June,
and for *Setaria* "emergence occurred over a short period, resulting in a prominent emergence peak". Large crabgrass:
Mid-Atlantic 18 May to 8 June, Ohio 1 June. Barnyardgrass: Arkansas late May, Ontario June, "though emergence may
occur for an extended period". Redroot pigweed: New York 28 April, Wisconsin 8 June, Maine 7 July and 18 August;
"This protracted emergence pattern of *Amaranthus* species has been well-described". All from the Northeast
emergence-peak review ([Frontiers in Agronomy 2022, 4:888664](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/agronomy/articles/10.3389/fagro.2022.888664/full), read, Table 1).

**Late and prolonged.** Waterhemp: Indiana first emergence about 425 GDD (base 48 °F), peak 500 to 600 (Purdue
2023); Iowa State's older figure is more than 350 GDD, and in Iowa trials it took seven weeks to 50% emergence and
ten more days to 90% (ISU, snippet). "Waterhemp plants emerge throughout the growing season, and a higher percentage
of plants emerge later in the season than most other summer annual weeds"
([GWC-13, Biology and Management of Waterhemp](https://extension.missouri.edu/media/wysiwyg/Extensiondata/Pub/pdf/miscpubs/mx1137.pdf), read).
Palmer amaranth in Texas: May to September (Keeling 2015). Johnsongrass from rhizomes and yellow and purple
nutsedge from tubers: no emergence-timing source retrieved; both are perennials that come up in the same patches
each year (section 5).

**What this means for a V2 to V5 flight** (our reading). Giant ragweed, common ragweed, lambsquarters and
velvetleaf will be up and, having emerged near planting, roughly the crop's age; foxtails will be a dense cohort of
small seedlings; waterhemp and Palmer amaranth may be absent or just emerging at V2 and well present by V5; a
clean-looking field at V3 says nothing about waterhemp in late June. Georgia frames it by days: control from 20 to
45 days after planting prevents yield loss, and weeds emerging after 45 DAP are a seed and harvest problem rather
than a yield one (UGA 2023); Virginia says weed-free for the first 4 to 6 weeks (PMG 2021).

The Iowa State 23-species emergence model (Werle et al. 2014, Weed Science 62:267-279; early, middle and late
groups by thermal time, ISU seedbank 1996 to 1999) is the reference table for the Corn Belt and was **not
retrieved** (BioOne and Cambridge returned empty pages).

## 4. What they look like from above, and what looks like corn

### 4.1 The published statements

Every maize UAV paper found says the same thing about colour. Peña et al. 2013, maize at 4 to 6 leaves, 2 cm
pixels, Madrid: "The spectral characteristics and general appearance of crop and weed plants are highly similar in
the early season"; weeds present were *Amaranthus blitoides* and *Sorghum halepense*; classification was by position
relative to the row, 86% overall accuracy, kappa 0.76, weed-cover R² 0.89; row edge definition was "strongly
affected by the presence of weed plants very close to or within the crop rows"
([PLoS ONE 8:e77151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3795646/), read).
Peña et al. 2015 (sunflower, but the method paper for the resolution rule): weed mapping is hardest "just when the
crop and weeds have similar appearance and spectral characteristics"; "at least four pixels are required to detect
the smallest objects"; the RGB camera "was ineffective on the earliest dates due to the small size of the weed
plants"; errors in weed-free zones "were due to the wrong classification of the crop-row edges as weeds", worse at
height; for individual weeds "the pixel size should be approximately 1–4 cm", for patches "5 cm or even greater"
([Sensors 15:5609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4435221/), read).
Louargant et al. 2018, maize and sugar beet in France at 6 mm per pixel from a 3 m pole (not a UAV): spectral-only
methods fail for three reasons, "the loading limit entails the use of a limited number of spectral bands; the
spectral information is affected by the uncontrolled field conditions (e.g., sun height, cloud cover, shadow, and
dust), and the leaf spectral reflectance changes with physiological stress"; their fix labels inter-row pixels as
weed and classifies in-row pixels by spectral similarity to those inter-row weeds, 89% weed detection combined
versus 79% spatial-only and 75% spectral-only, and "the method is suitable for wide-row crops and high spatial
resolution images (at least 6 mm/pix)" ([Remote Sensing 10:761](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/remotesensing/remotesensing-10-00761/article_deploy/remotesensing-10-00761.pdf), read).
Gao et al. 2018, early-season maize: Hough-transform rows for inter-row weeds, object-based random forest for
intra-row; "the ratio of vegetation length and width was the most significant feature", overall accuracy 0.945,
weed-density R² 0.895 ([Int J Appl Earth Obs 67:43-53](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/fusion-of-pixel-and-object-based-features-for-weed-mapping-using-/), abstract read; species and GSD not retrieved).
De Castro et al. 2018: DSM plus orthomosaic plus random forest, plant height from the DSM used as a feature to
pick training samples automatically, and "the ability to discriminate weeds was significantly affected by the
imagery spatial resolution and weed density" ([Remote Sensing 10:285](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/10/2/285), abstract, snippet).
Sapkota et al. 2022, North Dakota silage corn at 0.63 cm GSD: "we assumed that plants growing outside the corn
rows are weeds", with a 3.5 inch buffer each side of the detected row line
([arXiv 2301.07519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07519), read).

So the literature's cues, in order of use: (1) position relative to the row, used by every paper; (2) object shape,
length-to-width ratio (Gao); (3) height from a DSM (de Castro), which corn.md already shows is 4 to 17 cm noisy, the
size of a seedling; (4) spectral similarity to known inter-row weeds within the same image (Louargant), which needs
6 mm pixels; (5) texture and colour tone, used only as random-forest features alongside the others, never alone.
None of them names a species; the species classifiers found work at 0.3 to 0.5 cm (corn.md).

### 4.2 Grasses: the ones that are corn-shaped

Corn, shattercane, johnsongrass, the foxtails, barnyardgrass, fall panicum, crabgrass and Texas panicum are all
grasses with a single erect-to-spreading shoot at the seedling stage. The extension keys separate them by the
collar: "Grasses occurring as weeds are difficult to identify in their vegetative stages, particularly in the one-
to two-leaf seedling stage of growth", the collar is seen by "carefully pull[ing] back the blade" with a hand lens
([Missouri IPM1024, Identifying grass seedlings](https://extension.missouri.edu/media/wysiwyg/Extensiondata/Pub/pdf/agguides/pests/ipm1024.pdf), read).
Those cues (ligule membrane versus hair fringe, auricles, sheath hairs, flat versus round stem) are millimetre
features and do not exist at 1 cm per pixel. What might survive at 1 to 3 cm (our reading of the key's
dimensions):

The honest statement: at 1 to 3 cm a pixel a shattercane, johnsongrass or volunteer-corn plant standing in the
row is corn, and standing between rows it is "a grass that is not where corn was planted". That second fact is the
whole signal. Within-row sorghum-type grasses are only separable once they overtop the crop (johnsongrass and
shattercane both do, late) or by the DSM (noisy).

### 4.4 Cues the profile can use, and their basis

| Cue | Used by | Basis | Our limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Off-row position | Peña 2013, Gao 2018, de Castro 2018, Louargant 2018, Sapkota 2022 | every paper found | needs a found row; fails at headlands, point rows and guess rows (corn.md section A); fails for in-row weeds entirely |
| Object length-to-width ratio | Gao 2018 | "most significant feature" | distinguishes a grass tuft from a broadleaf rosette only once the object is tens of pixels |
| Height above the crop | de Castro 2018 (DSM feature); corn.md escapes | giant ragweed above corn late | 4 to 17 cm DSM error; useless for seedlings, good for late giant ragweed |
| Tone (pale mealy lambsquarters, grey hairy velvetleaf, glossy pigweeds) | extension keys | ground descriptions | never measured from the air in any source found |
| Patch shape and persistence (section 5) | Colbach 2000, Clay 2006, ISU | perennials and grasses sit still | a prior across flights, not a cue within one |
| In-image spectral similarity to known inter-row weeds | Louargant 2018 | 89% at 6 mm | 6 mm pixels, 2 to 5 times finer than ours |

## 5. Spatial pattern: patches, edges, rows

**Patchiness is the rule.** Cardina, Johnson and Sparrow 1997 reviewed the geostatistics and concluded that
"populations are aggregated at one or more scales", "few generalizations can be drawn except that populations are
aggregated", and "mean density alone is of limited value in estimating yield loss"
([Weed Science 45:364-373](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/abs/nature-and-consequence-of-weed-spatial-distribution/B11A6A5BA0E03E0EF502A5CB2A96AE81), abstract read).

**Patch size and shape.** Johnson, Mortensen and Gotway 1996, an eastern Nebraska corn and soybean field, 1992
and 1993, velvetleaf and common sunflower: aggregations were elliptical and oriented east-west (along the rows);
the north-south semivariogram changed slope at about 20 m; the east-west structure "remains consistent from 1992
to 1993" while the north-south extent contracted, read as smaller seedling patches in the second year
([Weed Science 44:704-710](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/abs/spatial-and-temporal-analysis-of-weed-seedling-populations-using-geostatistics/2FEA1390388BEE9A029B0BBEF041F89E), abstract read).
Dieleman's velvetleaf patches "were comprised of focal points of high density that decreased with distance from the
focal point. This pattern remained consistent before and after weed control and across years" (Nebraska
dissertation 1998, snippet; Dieleman and Mortensen 1999, Weed Research 39:455, not retrieved). Colbach, Forcella
and Johnson 2000, a 1.3 ha no-till row-crop field sampled at 410 grid points 1993 to 1997: "patches were elongated
along rows"; "aggregation of seedlings into patches was strongest for annuals" and for combine-dispersed species;
patches "were most persistent for perennials" and for species dispersing before harvest; for green foxtail, the
most abundant weed, this year's patches predicted next year's but no further
([Weed Science 48:366-377](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/abs/spatial-and-temporal-stability-of-weed-populations-over-five-years/6390B093C5E1098E61FEC6ACC9A16549), abstract read).
Clay et al. 2006, a 65 ha corn/soybean field over nine years: mean densities 12 to 131 annual grasses/m² and
under 1 to 37 common ragweed/m², medians 0 to 40 and 0; "grass patches were more stable in time, space, and
density than common ragweed patches"; a field-wide treatment would have been called in 6 of 9 years by the mean
and in none by the median; grid recommendations ranged from over 80% of the field (grasses, 3 of 4 soybean years)
to under 20% (ragweed, in the 2002 and 2004 corn years)
([Weed Science 54:380-390](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/spatial-distribution-temporal-stability-and-yield-loss-estimates-for-annual-grasses-and-common-ragweed-ambrosia-artimisiifolia-in-a-cornsoybean-production-field-over-nine-years/541CD0BCCCBE06702401D13BAD79528C), abstract read).
Iowa State's summary: "Perennial weed patches tend to be more stable than annual weed patches"; "the location of
annual weed patches within a field remains relatively stable from year to year" but their size "can vary widely";
a Nebraska 10-acre map found 59, 40 and 34% of the area free of sunflower, velvetleaf and foxtail respectively, and
"very little weed-free area" once the species were overlaid; velvetleaf and sunflower favoured high-organic-matter
low ground, annual grasses upland better-drained soils; a milkweed patch edge advanced under 1.5 ft a year
([ISU, spatial weed distribution](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/spatial-weed-distribution-can-it-be-used-improve-weed-management), read).
Heijting et al. 2007 (Dutch maize, two-dimensional patch stability) not retrieved. Typical patch sizes in metres
were not found stated anywhere beyond the 20 m semivariogram break; no source gives a distribution of patch sizes
for a corn field.

**Field edges.** No corn-specific edge study was found. General arable results (all snippets): species count and
seed density fall over the first 2 m from the edge (Land 2021); a UK headland study found the highest densities 1 to
5 m in from the edge; a US SARE report found more weeds near the edge than in the centre; Metcalfe et al. 2019 found
the share of typical arable weeds rising toward the field centre, with more perennial, non-arable species at the
edge; a six-year French study of 60 fields found edges richer and more stable. Read together: edges carry more
species, more perennials and often more plants, concentrated within the first few metres, and that is also where
the planter turns, so headland weediness and row-reading failure coincide (our reading).

**Within row versus between rows.** Donald, Johnson and Nelson 2004, three site-years of Missouri corn with giant
foxtail "the chief weed" and waterhemp the main broadleaf: midseason "total weed cover was as much as 20% lower
in-row (IR) than between-row (BR)" after a pre-emergence residual, because "the presence of row crops, such as
field corn, improves herbicidal control of weeds"
([Weed Technology 18:497-504](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-technology/article/abs/inrow-and-betweenrow-interference-by-corn-modifies-annual-weed-control-by-preemergence-residual-herbicide/2911A4101CCD503812E27B143AC42242), abstract read).
Banded pre-emergence plus one or two between-row mowings matched broadcast yields in the same group's work
(snippets). Between-row cultivation "controlled many late-emerging plants", and its decline favours waterhemp
(GWC-13). So after a pre-and-post programme the alley is the cleaner-measured and the more-weeded place at once:
whatever survives in the row is partly hidden by corn and partly suppressed by it, and whatever is in the alley is
what the imagery can count.

## 6. Herbicide programmes and what the field looks like afterwards

**How much, and what.** USDA NASS 2021 corn chemical-use survey, 19 states, 92.1% of the 93.4 million acres:
herbicides on 96% of planted acres; atrazine 65% of planted acres (average 1.054 lb/a), mesotrione 47%, glyphosate
isopropylamine salt 41% (other glyphosate salts are counted separately; secondary sources put all glyphosate at 79
to 90%, snippets), acetochlor 34%, S-metolachlor 27%; no-till or minimum till on 59%, crop rotation 79%, "using
pesticides with different mechanisms of action" 43%
([NASS chemical use highlights, corn 2021](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Surveys/Guide_to_NASS_Surveys/Chemical_Use/2021_Field_Crops/chemhighlights-corn.pdf), read).
Atrazine, mesotrione and the Group 15 chloroacetamides are the pre-emergence residual core; glyphosate and
glufosinate are the post pass in tolerant hybrids. No source gives the share of acres on pre-only, post-only or
pre-then-post (corn.md already notes this gap). GWC-13: "Under ideal conditions, preemergence programs may provide
full-season waterhemp control", and pre followed by post "will often provide more consistent weed control" than
either alone.

**Timing of the post pass against the V stage.** Labels set the ceiling, extension restates it: glyphosate
"broadcast to 24"; directed to 30""; Liberty "24"; V7"; Callisto and Halex GT "30" or 8 leaf"; Enlist "30" or V8
(Over the top); 30" to 48" (drop down nozzles)"; atrazine "12""; dicamba (Clarity) "8"" and Status "36""; Capreno
"V1 to V6 broadcast; V6 to V8 directed"; DiFlexx "Broadcast V6 or 36""; Resource "V10"
([UNL CropWatch, corn growth stage and post-emergence herbicides](https://cropwatch.unl.edu/consider-corn-growth-stage-when-applying-post-emergence-herbicides-weed-management/), read).
Virginia's table says the same in its own words: Callisto "to corn up to 30 inches tall (8-leaf stage)", atrazine
mixes "until corn reaches 12 inches tall", dicamba "before corn is 8 inches tall (5-leaf stage)", Basis Blend "from
spike stage through 2 collars (about 6 inches tall)", marestail "before marestail reaches 8 inches tall" (PMG 2021).
Weed-size targets are smaller than crop-size ceilings: "before weeds are 4 inches tall" (glyphosate), "broadleaf
weeds are less than 4 to 6 inches tall and grasses are less than 3 inches" (Capreno), "1–4 inches tall" (PMG). So
the post window closes at V8 or 30 in for most products and at V5 to V6 (12 in) for the atrazine and dicamba
mixes; after V8 only drop-nozzle directed sprays and a few late labels remain. Georgia: "2 applications of
glyphosate, applied approximately 21 and" (second interval not captured) days after planting, atrazine "before the
corn exceeds 12" in height", and "control of morningglory is almost impossible in our environment" (UGA 2023).

## 7. Herbicide resistance by region, for priors only

Counts for waterhemp (eight groups) and Palmer amaranth (nine) are in corn.md. Region by region:

- **Mid-Atlantic** (PMG 2021, read): triazine-resistant common lambsquarters, Palmer, redroot and smooth pigweed,
  horseweed, "scattered populations of barnyardgrass, giant foxtail, goosegrass, and suspected populations of
  common ragweed and velvetleaf"; ALS-resistant pigweeds, common and giant ragweed, chickweed, horseweed, Italian
  ryegrass, giant foxtail, johnsongrass, shattercane ("ALS-resistant johnsongrass and shattercane are a particular
  concern as only glyphosate and glufosinate are available for POST control in corn"); glyphosate-resistant Palmer
  amaranth, waterhemp, common and giant ragweed, horseweed. Virginia's own SPES-194: assume groups 2 and 9
  resistance in Palmer amaranth if unknown (snippet).
- **Corn Belt**: giant ragweed ALS resistance first in Indiana, Illinois, Ohio and Iowa in the late 1990s;
  glyphosate resistance "confirmed in 12 states across the Midwest and southern U.S."; both in Ohio, Minnesota,
  Missouri and Indiana; no other sites of action yet (Take Action 2025, read). Missouri: 58% of 144 waterhemp
  sites glyphosate-resistant; 12 of 27 giant ragweed farms (snippets). Glufosinate-resistant waterhemp reported in
  Illinois in January 2026; dicamba-resistant waterhemp in Illinois, Missouri, Nebraska and likely Iowa (snippets).
  Nearly all waterhemp is ALS-resistant (GWC-13).
- **Northern Plains**: kochia, waterhemp, common ragweed, horseweed, wild oat, green foxtail are NDSU's resistant
  list; 49 of 93 kochia populations PPO-resistant in a 2026 NDSU talk (snippets).
- **Southern Plains**: Texas confirmed resistance in order: perennial ryegrass 1989, barnyardgrass 1991, Palmer
  amaranth ALS 1993, kochia 1998, waterhemp 2006, johnsongrass 2009 (ALS), Palmer amaranth glyphosate 2011
  (Keeling 2015, read; sites of action partly garbled in the extracted table).
- **Southeast**: Georgia Palmer amaranth ALS 2000, glyphosate 2005, atrazine 2007/2008 (Macon, then Berrien and
  Dodge counties), ALS plus atrazine plus glyphosate 2010, PPO 2022; large crabgrass to Poast 2008; yellow nutsedge
  to Cadre 2018 (UGA 2023, Table 11, read).
- **Northeast**: glyphosate-resistant waterhemp confirmed in New York, January 2025 (snippet).

Use: a region's resistant species are the ones most likely to be standing green two weeks after a post pass. That
is a prior on "escape" patches, not a cue to a name.

## 9. Disambiguation rules and their limits

1. **Position before appearance.** A green object centred in the alley of a found 30 in row is a weed at any GSD
   we fly; this is the only crop-versus-weed rule the literature supports at 1 to 3 cm (Peña 2013, Gao 2018,
   de Castro 2018, Sapkota 2022). Limit: it says nothing about a grass in the row, and it fails wherever the row
   reader fails (headlands, point rows, guess rows, lodging), which is also where edge weeds concentrate.
2. **A grass in the row is corn until it overtops.** Shattercane, johnsongrass, volunteer corn, foxtails and
   barnyardgrass in the row are not separable from corn by colour, shape or texture at our resolution; the keys
   separate them by millimetre collar features. Limit: only johnsongrass and shattercane height late in the
   season, or same-place patches across years, give them away.
3. **Giant ragweed is the one broadleaf we can expect to resolve early and the only thing we can expect above the
   canopy late.** Its 4 to 8 in leaves and March to April emergence make it an object by V4 at 1 to 3 cm; its
   height makes it the escape. Limit: cocklebur and velvetleaf are also large early; the shape cues that separate
   them (lobed versus heart versus triangular) are ground cues until an object is tens of pixels.
4. **Pigweeds are a family, not a species, from the air.** Waterhemp, Palmer amaranth and redroot pigweed are
   "challenging, especially in the seedling stages" to tell apart on the ground (GWC-13); from above they are one
   glossy erect class. Region and season decide which name the operator is offered first (waterhemp north,
   Palmer south and spreading north), never the pixels.
5. **Season tells you what can be there.** Before about 400 GDD (base 48 °F) a clean alley does not mean a clean
   season; waterhemp has not started. After V8 new emergence matters for seed, not yield (corn.md). Limit: GDD
   thresholds are Indiana and Iowa numbers; Southern Palmer amaranth runs May to September.
6. **Patches are priors across flights, not cues within one.** Perennials (johnsongrass, nutsedge, bindweed,
   quackgrass, thistle) and annual grasses sit still year to year; broadleaf annual patches keep their location and
   change their size; patches run along the rows; the mean density overstates the field and the median
   understates it. Limit: no corn source gives a patch-size distribution; the 20 m figure is one Nebraska field.
7. **Edges are different.** Expect more species, more perennials and often more plants in the first 1 to 5 m, with
   the share of typical arable annuals rising toward the centre. Limit: no corn-specific edge study; all snippets.
8. **The herbicide programme sets what survives.** A pre-residual field looks clean to V3 except for large-seeded
   deep emergers; the post window closes at V8 or 30 in; whatever is green two to three weeks after the post pass
   is an escape, and the region's resistant list says which species it is likeliest to be. Limit: a flight within
   days of spraying shows green weeds that are already dying, and bleached HPPD-treated plants drop out of a green
   mask (corn.md).
9. **Never a name from the map.** Everything above is a prior for ordering the operator's list or for a sentence
   like "an erect glossy broadleaf in the alley, in a region and month where waterhemp is the common one"; the
   catalog rule that a name is only ever the operator's statement stands.

## 10. Gaps and uncertainties

- WSSA per-state and per-region tables for corn (2020) and the 2023 grass-crop results: wssa.net refused every
  fetch; the regional ranking the brief asked for is reconstructed from state sources, not from the survey.
- Werle et al. 2014's early, middle and late groups for 23 species (the Corn Belt emergence table) not retrieved.
- Kochia, sicklepod, nutsedge and johnsongrass emergence timing relative to corn: not retrieved.
- No source gives weed size at corn V2, V3, V4 or V5; the table's size column rests on habit descriptions and
  Gower 2003's 10 cm by V4 for crop-synchronous cohorts.
- No corn-specific field-edge study; no patch-size distribution for a corn field; Dieleman and Mortensen 1999 and
  Heijting et al. 2007 not retrieved.
- Gao 2018 species list and GSD, de Castro 2018 sites and height thresholds: full texts refused.
- Tone cues (mealy lambsquarters, hairy velvetleaf, glossy pigweeds) have never been measured from the air in any
  source found; they are ground-key descriptions.
- Pre-only versus pre-then-post versus post-only acreage shares: still not found (also a corn.md gap).
- The Turkish picture is three provinces and a thesis; no national maize survey; the TAGEM guidance was not
  re-opened. The Sakarya maize survey was seen only as a snippet.
- The web-search budget was exhausted part way through; later items are from known URLs only, so Palmer
  amaranth emergence periodicity studies, kochia biology, volunteer-corn extension pages and the NDSU corn material
  were not searched for.
- Survey "troublesome" rankings are expert opinion on control difficulty; they correlate with resistance, not with
  how often a weed is on the ground (WSSA's own definition). Field counts (Wisconsin 2012, Germany 2000 to 2004)
  rank lambsquarters and grasses higher than the opinion lists do.
